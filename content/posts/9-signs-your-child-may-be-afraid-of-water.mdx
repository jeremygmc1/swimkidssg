Many parents think fear of water is obvious. They imagine a child crying, refusing to enter the pool, or clinging tightly to a parent before swimming lessons begin.

But fear does not always look that way.

Some children walk happily to the pool and even smile during class, yet still feel anxious once they enter the water. Others may enjoy splashing around in shallow areas but become uncomfortable when they are asked to float, put their face underwater, or move away from the pool edge.

In Singapore, where many families have access to condo pools, public swimming complexes, and early swimming lessons, parents often assume that frequent exposure automatically creates confidence. While regular exposure can help, being physically around water is not always the same as feeling emotionally comfortable in it.

The good news is that fear of water is common and, in many cases, temporary. The earlier parents recognise the signs, the easier it becomes to build positive experiences and confidence.

Here are nine signs parents often miss.

## 1. Your child holds onto you tightly

One of the most obvious but commonly overlooked signs is excessive clinging.

Some children will hold tightly onto a parent, instructor, or pool wall even when they are standing in shallow water. They may constantly ask to be carried or insist on holding someone's hand throughout the lesson.

Children naturally seek security when they feel uncertain. If they seem unable to let go even after some time in the pool, fear may be playing a role.

## 2. Your child avoids putting their face in the water

Many children dislike getting water into their eyes or nose initially. That alone does not automatically indicate fear.

However, if your child repeatedly refuses activities involving bubbles, face wetting, or brief submersion while happily doing other activities, they may be feeling anxious rather than simply disliking the sensation.

Parents sometimes unintentionally force this step too early by saying:

_"Come on, just put your face in for one second."_

For a child who feels uncomfortable, one second can feel much longer.

## 3. Your child repeatedly asks for reassurance

Questions such as:

- "Are you holding me?"
- "What if I sink?"
- "You're not letting go, right?"
- "Are we going into deep water?"

can be signs of uncertainty.

Children often ask the same questions multiple times because they are trying to reduce anxiety and predict what will happen next.

Reassurance helps, but repeated reassurance seeking can sometimes indicate underlying fear.

## 4. Your child avoids certain parts of the pool

Some children appear comfortable until the activity changes.

You may notice that they:

- Stay close to steps
- Refuse to move into slightly deeper areas
- Avoid activities in the centre of the pool
- Move back toward the wall whenever possible

Parents often think:

_"But my child loves the water."_

The reality may be that they love the parts that feel safe and predictable.

## 5. Your child suddenly becomes quiet

Not every fearful child cries.

Some children react by becoming unusually silent.

A child who is normally energetic may suddenly stop talking, avoid eye contact, or become very serious during lessons.

Children process anxiety differently. Some express it loudly while others become withdrawn.

## 6. Your child starts making excuses before lessons

Watch for patterns.

Your child may suddenly say:

- "I'm tired."
- "I don't want to go today."
- "My stomach hurts."
- "Can we skip swimming?"

Occasional reluctance is normal.

But if these comments appear regularly before swimming sessions, there may be more happening beneath the surface.

## 7. Your child suddenly regresses

Parents sometimes feel confused when a child who was previously doing well suddenly refuses activities.

For example:

- They used to kick independently
- They previously floated comfortably
- They suddenly become clingy again

Regression is often frustrating for parents because it feels like progress is disappearing.

In reality, regression can happen for many reasons:

- A frightening experience
- Water entering the nose unexpectedly
- A long break from lessons
- Changes in confidence

Temporary setbacks are often a normal part of learning.

## 8. Your child constantly stays close to the wall

The pool wall becomes a "safe zone" for many children.

You may notice them reaching for it whenever possible or swimming only along the edges.

The behaviour itself is understandable. Children naturally seek security.

However, if your child refuses to move away from the wall entirely, they may not yet trust themselves in the water.

## 9. Your child becomes upset before swimming even begins

Sometimes the strongest signs happen before entering the pool.

Parents may notice:

- Mood changes before lessons
- Complaints on swimming days
- Nervous behaviour while changing
- Resistance during preparation

Children often anticipate experiences emotionally long before they actually happen.

Pay attention to what happens before the lesson rather than only during it.

## What parents should avoid

When parents see fear, the natural instinct is often to fix it immediately.

Unfortunately, some common reactions can unintentionally increase pressure.

Avoid:

### Comparing your child with others

Statements like:

_"Look at your brother."_

or

_"That child is younger than you."_

may increase anxiety instead of confidence.

### Forcing progress

Pushing children into activities before they feel ready can create stronger resistance later.

### Dismissing emotions

Saying:

_"Don't be scared."_

usually does not remove fear.

Children want to feel understood before they feel brave.

### Using rewards for every step

Encouragement can help, but relying heavily on treats or rewards may shift attention away from genuine confidence building.

## How swim coaches help children build confidence gradually

Building confidence usually starts long before learning strokes.

Many coaches focus first on helping children feel safe and comfortable in the water.

This often includes:

- Introducing activities gradually
- Using games and play
- Building trust
- Celebrating small wins
- Allowing children to progress at their own pace

Children who feel emotionally safe often become physically confident over time.

## Final thoughts

Fear of water does not mean a child cannot learn swimming.

It also does not mean parents have done anything wrong.

Many confident swimmers once started by holding tightly to the pool wall or refusing to wet their faces.

Progress often begins with recognising small signs and responding patiently.
